Capacity Network Design

A greedy algorithm to explore interactions between the competing operational objectives in deciding how blood donation collection appointments (slots) should be distributed geographically.

Overview

Themes / Analytical methods

R, Multi-Criterion Decision Analysis, Computational statistics/Numerical methods, leaflet choropleth geo-mapping, conveying abstract concepts to decision-makers

Business Problem

Problem statement

The organisation’s current operational setup for blood donation collection is not optimised. Appointments (slots) are not optimally distributed in either a spatial or temporal sense i.e. people who are willing and able to donate blood cannot always find a slot near them, at a suitable time. What would the collection capacity network looked like if we rebuilt it from scratch in the optimal way?

Academic Aim

A numerical method by way of a greedy algorithm that, given a set of organisational objectives along with relative weights or priorities, allocates a given number of appointments geographically so as to maximise the likelihood of meeting those objectives.

Scoping and planning

  • I decided to consider an optimisation problem with respect to only the spatial distributiion of slots, as a first step. I recognised that the temporal aspect could be dealt with independently, because I had no reason to believe that the optimal temporal distribution should be significantly different between geographical areas - as such the problem could be reduced to two independent optimisation problems, of which I would tackle the spatial one in the first instance.
  • I recognised this as an application for Multi-Criterion Decision Analysis (MCDA), especially considering the difficulty of expressing different organisational objectives in comparable terms e.g. collecting enough blood from Black African and Black Caribbean donors, and minimising the distance collected blood needs to travel to manufacturing sites

Technical choices

  • I recognised that the optimal solution would be a function of the ordering or weighting of the competing orgnisational objectives, and so chose to use a computational approach of an algorithm that incrementally added capacity to the next-best location at each step (greedy) by using an objective function that was a weighted average of “monetised” or otherwise quantified organisational objectives
  • I decided that a solution that conveyed the above mechanic, that the weighting/priority strongly determined the optimum, was more important to convey to decision-makers than converging on a contrived or otherwise arbitrary “objective” optimum. As such, I felt justified in using an approach that can sometimes miss the global optimum.
  • The computational approach with the weighted objective function also allowed me to
    • demonstrate with fine control how tweaking or varying the weighted shifted the optimum in the direction of different extremes, to non-technical decision makers
    • compute and compare different scenarios side by side
  • As a proof of concept (POC) I developed a Shiny app with inputs for parameters to demonstrate the reduction of the problem down to the weighted objectives

Validation

hypothetical so want to make sure outputs make sense and that varying paramters in one direction cause sensible movemenets in output.

Impact

understood tradeoff and need for prioritisation.
